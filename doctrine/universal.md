# Universal Doctrine
### Foundational Principles of DIA International

**Project:** DIA International  
**Architecture:** [Jurisdiction3D](https://github.com/democratic-alliance/jurisdiction3d)  
**Classification:** Foundational Doctrine  
**Version:** 0.1.0  
**Status:** Draft

## 1. Purpose

The Universal Doctrine establishes the foundational principles governing the existence, recognition, autonomy, interaction, and jurisdictional relationships of subjects.

It provides a conceptual foundation for the DIA architecture.

Its purpose is to establish a consistent relationship between:

- Existence
- Subjectivity
- Identity
- Agency
- Action
- Space
- Authority
- Jurisdiction
- Normativity
- Evidence

The doctrine does not establish a universal sovereign authority.

It establishes principles for the coexistence and interaction of independently existing subjects and normative systems.

## 2. Primacy of Existence

Existence is primary.

Recognition does not create existence.

A subject does not acquire the right to exist merely because another subject, institution, government, or protocol recognizes it.

Recognition is an acknowledgment of independently existing reality.

**Principle:**

Existence precedes recognition.

## 3. Subjecthood

A subject is an independently existing entity capable of being distinguished from its environment and participating in relationships with other entities.

Subjecthood must not be confused with:

- Identity;
- Legal personality;
- Citizenship;
- Authority;
- Recognition;
- Organizational membership.

These concepts may be related, but they are not interchangeable.

## 4. Identity and Recognition

Identity describes the distinguishable characteristics through which a subject can be identified.

Recognition establishes whether another subject or system acknowledges a particular identity claim.

Neither identity nor recognition creates the underlying existence of the subject.

**Principle:**

Identity represents. Recognition acknowledges. Existence precedes both.

## 5. Agency and Autonomy

Agency is the capacity to initiate or participate in actions.

Autonomy concerns the subject's ability to determine its own internal decisions and exercise its agency.

External systems may establish constraints on actions where legitimate authority and applicable rules exist.

Such constraints must not automatically be interpreted as unrestricted authority over the subject.

## 6. The Subject–Action Boundary

The subject and its actions are architecturally distinct.

A subject exists independently of any particular action.

An action may be evaluated under applicable legal, contractual, organizational, ethical, or protocol rules.

**Fundamental distinction:**

```text
SUBJECT ≠ ACTION
EXISTENCE ≠ AUTHORITY
IDENTITY ≠ JURISDICTION
CONTROL ≠ CONSTRAINT
```

This distinction forms the conceptual foundation of Jurisdiction3D.

## 7. Space and Interaction

Subjects exist and interact within physical, social, institutional, digital, and conceptual spaces.

Space establishes the context in which interactions occur.

Interactions may generate obligations, permissions, restrictions, and jurisdictional relationships.

No single spatial context automatically eliminates the existence or relevance of other contexts.

## 8. Authority and Legitimacy

Authority must have an identifiable source.

Legitimacy must not be inferred solely from:

- Technical capability;
- Institutional recognition;
- Possession of resources;
- Control over infrastructure;
- Identity claims;
- Delegated privileges.

Authority must be established through an applicable normative or legal framework.

## 9. Jurisdictional Pluralism

Multiple normative systems may coexist.

Their applicability depends on their sources, scope, participants, and governing contexts.

No normative system automatically becomes universal merely because it is technically implemented or institutionally adopted.

Mandatory local law remains applicable within its legitimate scope.

## 10. Delegation and Non-Escalation

Delegation transfers limited authority to perform specified actions.

It does not transfer unrestricted control over the delegating subject.

Delegated authority must remain:

- Explicit;
- Scoped;
- Attributable;
- Verifiable;
- Non-escalating;
- Revocable where appropriate.

No delegated authority may silently manufacture additional authority.

## 11. Evidence and Verifiability

Claims about identity, authority, actions, and decisions should be supported by verifiable evidence wherever feasible.

Evidence establishes what can be demonstrated.

It does not independently establish the legitimacy of the authority or normative framework behind an action.

**Principle:**

Proof establishes facts. Normativity establishes applicable rules. Legitimacy requires an independently justified source of authority.

## 12. Privacy and Protected Domains

Verification does not require unrestricted disclosure.

The architecture should preserve the distinction between:

- What must be proven;
- What may be disclosed;
- What must remain protected.

Privacy is an architectural requirement rather than an optional convenience.

## 13. Contestability

No system should assume that its own decisions are inherently infallible.

Subjects must retain access to applicable mechanisms for challenging identity claims, authority, evidence, procedures, and decisions.

A record of disagreement is part of the system's evidentiary history.

## 14. Constitutional Invariants

The Universal Doctrine establishes the following initial invariants:

1. Existence precedes recognition.
2. Recognition does not create subjecthood.
3. Identity is distinct from existence.
4. Subjecthood is distinct from legal personality.
5. Agency is distinct from authority.
6. The subject is distinct from its actions.
7. Jurisdiction constrains actions within its legitimate scope.
8. Authority requires an identifiable source.
9. Delegation does not imply surrender.
10. Delegated authority cannot silently escalate.
11. Evidence does not independently establish legitimacy.
12. Verification must respect protected domains.
13. Normative pluralism requires distinguishable sources and scopes.
14. Consequential decisions must remain contestable.

## 16. Closing Principle

The fundamental question of DIA International is not who should possess unrestricted control.

It is how independently existing subjects can interact under legitimate constraints without surrendering their fundamental autonomy.

Therefore:

**Existence precedes recognition.**

**Identity does not create existence.**

**Authority must be justified.**

**Jurisdiction must be bounded.**

**Delegation must be scoped.**

**Evidence must be verifiable.**

**And no system may silently transform limited authority into unrestricted control.**
