## 1. Purpose

The International Doctrine establishes the principles governing relations between sovereign states, international organizations, institutions, communities, and individuals.

It extends the Universal Doctrine into the domain of international relations.

Its purpose is to establish an architecture in which international cooperation is based on:

- Fundamental rights;
- Subject sovereignty;
- Jurisdictional boundaries;
- Verifiable commitments;
- Institutional accountability;
- Normative pluralism;
- Non-escalation of delegated authority;
- Procedural fairness.

The Doctrine seeks to reduce dependence on discretionary political assurances by introducing verifiable institutional and technological mechanisms.

## 2. International Subjecthood

International relations involve multiple categories of subjects.

These include:

- States;
- International organizations;
- Autonomous institutions;
- Legal entities;
- Communities;
- Individuals.

Their legal status, rights, obligations, and capacities may differ.

Recognition by another subject does not independently create the existence of the recognized subject.

Likewise, the absence of recognition does not automatically eliminate the underlying rights or interests of individuals and communities.

**Principle:**

Existence precedes recognition. Subjecthood must not be reduced to institutional recognition.

## 3. Sovereignty and Autonomy

Sovereignty describes a subject's authority within its legitimate domain.

It does not establish unrestricted authority over every subject or activity within a geographical territory.

International sovereignty must coexist with:

- Fundamental human rights;
- Applicable international law;
- The autonomy of individuals;
- Legitimate jurisdictional boundaries;
- International obligations.

No sovereignty claim automatically overrides fundamental rights.

No international organization automatically acquires unrestricted authority over its members.

## 4. Fundamental Rights Architecture (L0–L2)

International cooperation requires a distinguishable hierarchy of normative obligations.

DIA adopts a three-layer conceptual architecture.

### L0♥️ — Fundamental Rights

The foundational layer.

Includes fundamental protections such as:

- Right to life;
- Freedom from torture;
- Human dignity;
- Personal integrity;
- Fundamental identity protections;
- Basic individual autonomy.

L0 establishes constraints that cannot be casually traded for economic or political advantages.

### L1 — Institutional and Legal Integrity

The institutional layer.

Includes:

- Rule of law;
- Judicial independence;
- Institutional accountability;
- Freedom of expression;
- Protection against arbitrary censorship;
- Transparency of public institutions;
- Procedural guarantees.

L1 establishes the institutional conditions required to protect fundamental rights.

### L2 — Economic and International Cooperation

The cooperation layer.

Includes:

- Trade;
- Investments;
- Technology exchange;
- Energy cooperation;
- Financial relationships;
- Infrastructure;
- Scientific and industrial cooperation.

L2 defines the conditions under which international cooperation may be established and maintained.

**Principle:**

Economic cooperation must not become a mechanism for purchasing exemptions from fundamental rights obligations.

## 5. Conditional International Cooperation

International cooperation is not necessarily unconditional.

Participating subjects may establish explicit conditions governing:

- Access to markets;
- Financial infrastructure;
- Technology transfers;
- Investment mechanisms;
- Shared information systems;
- Institutional cooperation.

Conditions must have an identifiable normative basis, a defined scope, and a verifiable implementation mechanism.

Restrictions must be proportionate to their legitimate purpose.

They must not arbitrarily deprive individuals of fundamental protections.

## 6. Verifiable Commitments

International commitments should be capable of independent verification.

DIA recognizes several complementary mechanisms:

### 6.1 Cryptographic Verification

Cryptographic mechanisms can establish the integrity and authenticity of records, signatures, and transactions.

### 6.2 Zero-Knowledge Proofs

Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKP) enable verification of specific statements without requiring unrestricted disclosure of underlying information.

### 6.3 Decentralized Oracles

Independent information sources may provide evidence concerning externally observable events.

Oracle integrity, provenance, independence, and potential conflicts of interest must themselves be verifiable.

### 6.4 Event Sourcing

International commitments and transactions may be represented through an immutable or tamper-evident history of events.

### 6.5 REA Model

The Resource-Event-Agent model provides a conceptual framework for recording:

- Resources;
- Economic and institutional events;
- Participating agents.

These mechanisms improve accountability but do not independently establish the legitimacy of a political or legal decision.

## 7. Jurisdictional Boundaries

International actions must be evaluated within their applicable jurisdictional contexts.

A subject's authority over an action must be distinguishable from its authority over the subject performing that action.

DIA applies the following distinction:

SUBJECT ≠ ACTION

EXISTENCE ≠ AUTHORITY

SOVEREIGNTY ≠ UNLIMITED CONTROL

RECOGNITION ≠ LEGITIMACY

Technical capability does not independently establish jurisdiction.

## 8. Accountability and Enforcement

Enforcement mechanisms must be based on identifiable rules and verifiable evidence.

A legitimate enforcement procedure should include:

1. Identification of the applicable obligation.
2. Establishment of the relevant jurisdiction.
3. Collection and verification of evidence.
4. Attribution of responsibility.
5. Assessment of the nature and severity of the violation.
6. Proportionate response.
7. Independent review and appeal where applicable.

Automated mechanisms may execute previously authorized decisions.

They must not independently manufacture legal authority or determine contested facts without adequate safeguards.

## 9. Non-Escalation Principle

Delegated authority must remain limited to its original scope.

No international organization, technical protocol, oracle network, or automated enforcement mechanism may silently expand its jurisdiction through the exercise of delegated powers.

Delegation does not imply unrestricted sovereignty.

Technical execution does not create normative legitimacy.

## 10. International Cooperation Status

DIA recognizes that international relationships may exist under different institutional and legal conditions.

The following categories provide a conceptual classification:

### 10.1 Democratic Peer

A subject participating in institutional cooperation under mutually established democratic and accountability standards.

### 10.2 Associated Partner

A subject participating in cooperation under defined transitional or conditional arrangements.

### 10.3 Monitored Entity

A subject whose cooperation is subject to additional verification, safeguards, and conditional mechanisms.

### 10.4 Restricted Relationship

A relationship subject to proportionate restrictions under applicable legal and institutional procedures.

These categories describe cooperation arrangements, not the intrinsic worth or fundamental rights of their subjects.

Classification must be evidence-based, reviewable, and subject to established procedures.

## 11. Technological Sovereignty

Technological infrastructure must not become an unaccountable source of political authority.

International technological cooperation should preserve:

- Data integrity;
- Cryptographic verifiability;
- Privacy;
- Interoperability;
- Auditability;
- Security;
- Institutional accountability.

No technological system should acquire unrestricted control merely because other participants depend on its infrastructure.

## 12. Privacy and Information Sovereignty

Verification and transparency must coexist with legitimate confidentiality.

International cooperation should distinguish between:

- Public information;
- Protected personal information;
- Institutional confidential information;
- State secrets;
- Verifiable commitments.

The ability to prove compliance should not automatically require unrestricted disclosure.

Privacy-preserving verification is therefore an important architectural requirement.

## 13. Dispute Resolution

International disagreements should be addressed through procedures that distinguish:

- Factual disputes;
- Interpretative disputes;
- Jurisdictional disputes;
- Normative contradictions;
- Institutional conflicts.

The existence of disagreement must not automatically be interpreted as non-compliance.

DIA supports mechanisms for independent review, evidence-based assessment, mediation, and applicable judicial procedures.

## 14. Constitutional Invariants

The International Doctrine establishes the following initial invariants:

1. Fundamental rights are not conditional on political recognition.
2. Sovereignty is bounded by applicable fundamental obligations.
3. Authority requires an identifiable normative source.
4. Jurisdiction must be explicitly scoped.
5. Economic cooperation cannot override fundamental rights.
6. International commitments should be verifiable.
7. Evidence must have identifiable provenance.
8. Verification must respect legitimate privacy protections.
9. Enforcement must be proportionate and procedurally justified.
10. Delegated authority cannot silently escalate.
11. Technical capability does not establish political legitimacy.
12. International classifications must remain reviewable.
13. Disputes require identifiable resolution procedures.
14. No institutional or technological system possesses automatic universal sovereignty.

## 16. Closing Declaration

International cooperation must not depend exclusively on trust in political actors.

Nor should it depend exclusively on trust in technological infrastructure.

A sustainable international architecture requires independently verifiable commitments, legitimate authority, bounded jurisdiction, fundamental rights, and accountable enforcement.

The objective is not to establish a universal sovereign.

The objective is to establish conditions under which independently existing subjects can cooperate without surrendering their fundamental autonomy.

**Existence precedes recognition.**

**Rights precede political convenience.**

**Authority requires justification.**

**Jurisdiction requires boundaries.**

**Cooperation requires accountability.**

**Verification requires evidence.**

**And no delegated power may silently become unrestricted sovereignty.**
